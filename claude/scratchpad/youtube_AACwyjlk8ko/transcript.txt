Some people can walk up to a machine
they have never seen before, a dryer
that spins but won't heat, a car that
clicks and refuses to start, a laptop
that dies at 40% and within a few
minutes they put their finger on the one
part that failed. They didn't read the
manual. They may never have owned that
model. And yet they move through it as
if they built it themselves. Psychology
has a name for what's happening in their
heads and it has surprisingly little to
do with their hands. It's called
mechanical intelligence, the ability to
understand how things work by building a
working model of them in your mind and
running it. Once you understand how this
kind of intelligence works, you'll see
why some people can fix anything and why
so many of us freeze the moment
something stops working. Most people
have a few labels ready for these
people. They're handy. They're good with
their hands. Sometimes, with a little
pity in the voice, they're not really
book smart but great with that kind of
stuff. And if you ask how to become one
of them, you get the usual advice. Watch
a tutorial or accept that some people
are just born that way. But the hands
are the last part of the process to
move. By the time a fixer picks up a
screwdriver, most of the real work has
already happened somewhere you can't
see. What looks like a knack in the
fingers is one of the most demanding
kinds of thinking a person can do. And
it's the kind our schools almost never
test for. Back in 1920, the psychologist
Edward Thorndike split human
intelligence into three kinds: abstract,
social, and mechanical. The first is
roughly what IQ tests came to measure.
The second grew into an entire industry
of books and seminars. The third, the
intelligence of things, we mostly
forgot. Decades later, Vanderbilt
researchers followed hundreds of gifted
13-year-olds for more than 30 years.
Their spatial ability, the skill of
turning objects around in the mind,
predicted who would later earn patents
and publish research beyond what their
math and verbal scores explained. Yet,
as David Lubinski, who led the study,
pointed out the usual searches for
gifted kids miss about half of the top
1% in spatial ability. And the educator
Mike Rose, after years of watching
plumbers, electricians, and carpenters
at work, argued that our old line
between hand work and brain work hides
an enormous amount of thinking.

In 1943,
a young Scottish psychologist named
Kenneth Craik published a short book
with a very big idea. He proposed that
the mind carries a small-scale model of
the outside world, a miniature reality
it can run in private. With that model,
you can try out different moves, see
where they lead, and choose the best one
before you touch anything at all. Craik
died 2 years later at just 31, but his
idea became the foundation of what
psychologists now call mental models.
Everyone carries mental models. In a
person with high mechanical
intelligence, though, the model of a
machine behaves less like a photograph
and more like a film. It runs. The
cognitive psychologist Mary Hegarty at
the University of California, Santa
Barbara, spent years studying exactly
this. She showed people simple diagrams
of pulley systems, ropes, and wheels,
and a hanging weight, and asked
questions like, "If you pull this rope,
which way does that pulley turn?" Then
she tracked where their eyes went while
they worked it out. Nobody pictured the
whole machine moving at once. Their eyes
traveled along the system one connection
at a time. This rope pulls this wheel.
This wheel turns that one. That one
lifts the weight. Hegarty called it
mental animation, and the people who did
best had one particular strengths,
spatial visualization, the ability to
hold one step steady in the mind while
running the next. Here's the simplest
way to picture it. Every machine is a
relay race. A switch hands the job to a
wire, the wire hands it to a motor, the
motor hands it to a belt, and the belt
hands it to the drum that finally spins
your clothes.
When something breaks, almost always one
runner somewhere has dropped the baton.
Most people look at a broken machine and
see a dead object. The fixer sees the
race and starts asking where the baton
went missing. Watch these people closely
and three signs keep showing up. Sign
one, they see parts where everyone else
sees a name. When a table wobbles, most
people see a table. The fixer sees four
legs, one of them a few millimeters
short, and a folded coaster on the
counter that happens to be exactly the
right thickness. Psychologists call the
trap that catches everyone else
functional fixedness. Once an object has
a name, the name tells you what it's for
and it quietly hides everything else it
could be. In 2012, the psychologist Tony
McCaffrey at the University of
Massachusetts tested a simple way around
it. He taught people to break every
object into its parts and describe each
part without implying a use. A candle
stops being a candle. It becomes a stick
of wax and a length of string. In one
classic puzzle, that string, the wick,
is exactly what you need to tie two
metal rings together. People trained
this way solved the puzzles more than
eight times out of 10, far more often
than people who weren't. Fixers do this
without being taught. Names slide off
things and what's left is material,
shape, and what that material could do.
The price is a little funny and a little
lonely. They can never look at anything
as just itself. Every drawer in their
home slowly fills with screws, springs,
and things that might be useful someday.
The people around them see clutter. The
fixer sees inventory. Sign two, they
follow the job instead of chasing the
symptom. Picture a car that won't start
on a cold morning. Most people jump
straight to a guess. It's probably the
battery. So, they buy a new one, install
it, turn the key, and hear the exact
same click. The fixer asks smaller
questions first. Do the dashboard lights
come on? Does the engine try to turn
over? Or is there just one click? Each
question checks one point along the path
the power is supposed to take. In the
1970s, the Danish engineer Jens
Rasmussen studied technicians hunting
for faults in electronic equipment and
described two strategies. The first he
called symptomatic, match the symptom to
a fault you've seen before. It's fast
and it works beautifully as long as the
problem is familiar. The second he
called topographic, set the symptom
aside and follow the route the system
takes when it's healthy point by point
until the good signal turns bad. This is
the fixer following the baton. They
start where the race begins and watch
each handoff until they find the runner
standing there with empty hands. And
this is the real answer to the question
in the title. Matching symptoms only
works on problems you've met before.
Following the path works on almost
anything because every machine, even one
you've never laid eyes on, has a path.
Their memory supplies a few good first
guesses. The machine itself supplies the
rest. Ask them how they knew and many of
them can't quite say. Something in the
click sounded wrong. A screw turned with
the wrong kind of resistance. The
philosopher Michael Polanyi had a line
for this. We can know more than we can
tell.
Years of following paths leave knowledge
in the ears and fingertips that never
gets translated into words. The cost of
this sign is time and other people's
patience. Following the path is slower
than guessing and to the people waiting
it can look stubborn, even obsessive.
Sometimes it is. More often it's the
reason the problem stays fixed. Sign
three, they open it. A coffee machine
stops working. For most people, it's
inside might as well be a locked room
with a quiet fear attached to the door.
If I open this, I'll make it worse and
everyone will see I had no idea what I
was doing. So, the machine goes in the
trash or sits in a corner of the counter
for a year. The fixer turns it over,
finds the screws, and takes a photo
before removing each one. Every time
they open something, they add another
working model to a private library of
how things work, and that library
compounds. In 1984, the researchers
David Kirsh and Susan Bovair taught
people to operate an unfamiliar control
panel dressed up as the weapons console
of a starship. Some learned only the
steps. Others first got a simple picture
of how power flowed through the device.
That second group learned faster,
remembered better, and most remarkably,
worked out correct procedures nobody had
ever taught them. One real understanding
of how a thing works can produce dozens
of answers you were never given. The
philosopher Matthew Crawford, who left a
think tank job in Washington to run a
motorcycle repair shop, argues that this
work demands an unusual humility. The
machine has no interest in your theory
or your title, and you have to be
willing to be wrong in front of it. It's
an argument from the workbench rather
than the lab, but anyone who has
repaired anything will recognize it. And
here's the part nobody sees. The
confidence you admire in a fixer was
bought with broken things. They have
stripped screws, snapped plastic clips,
cracked casings, and made at least one
thing permanently worse. Many people who
can fix anything have broken more
objects than you have ever owned. They
just kept opening. But, there's a deeper
price, and it shows up far away from any
machine. When you spend years hunting
for the dropped baton, you start hunting
for it everywhere. A friend calls after
a terrible week, and before they finish
their second sentence, the fixer is
already three hand-offs into a
diagnosis. Was it your manager? Have you
tried talking to HR? Every question
comes from care, and it can still land
wrong. Researchers who followed couples
while one partner prepared for the bar
exam found that the support that helped
most was often the support the stressed
partner barely noticed. Obvious help
sometimes carried a hidden cost because
being helped can feel like being told
you're struggling. Later studies suggest
it depends on whether the help matches
what the person actually needs. But the
risk is real. Sometimes nothing in the
person is broken at all. They wanted
company, and they got a repair. A mind
built to restore things can quietly cost
them closeness. So, what if you're not
one of them? What if you're the one who
freezes the moment something stops
working? Here's the first thing to know.
You can't get there by memorizing fixes.
A thousand repair videos will still
leave you lost the moment something
fails in a way the video didn't cover.
When the researchers Morris and Rouse
reviewed years of troubleshooting
studies in 1985, they found that
teaching people the theory of how a
system works did not, by itself, produce
good troubleshooters. What helped was
guidance on how to use what they knew.
And knowledge of the actual task
mattered more than raw aptitude.
Memorized routes fail the moment one
road closes. A map lets you find a way
you've never taken. The good news is
that the ability underneath all of this
can be trained. In 2013, a team led by
David Uttal at Northwestern University
pooled 217 studies on training spatial
skills. Practice improved those skills,
the gains held up over time, and they
carried over to tasks people had never
practiced. The people who started out
weakest improved the most, which brings
us to the hardest part of this video. If
you've spent your life saying, "I'm just
not a mechanical person," take an honest
look at what that sentence does for you.
It sounds like a description of who you
are. In practice, it works like a lid.
You closed it a long time ago, maybe
after one embarrassing moment in front
of someone who knew more, and you've
kept it shut ever since because as long
as you never open anything, you never
have to feel what it's like to not know.
What that sentence really protects you
from is the first 5 minutes of being a
beginner, and the price is a lifetime of
waiting for someone else to fix things
for you.
So, here are three ways to start
thinking like someone who can fix
anything. Principle one, say the chain
out loud before you touch anything.
Before you open or replace anything,
describe how the thing is supposed to
work step-by-step out loud or on paper.
I press the button, power reaches the
heater, the heater warms the water, the
pump pushes the water through the
coffee. This is Hegarty's mental
animation done on purpose, and pay
attention to the moment you get stuck.
The first step you can't describe is
exactly where your model has a gap, and
very often, it's exactly where to look.
Principle two,
test the middle first. When you don't
know which step failed, skip your
favorite suspect and test somewhere in
the middle.
If a lamp won't turn on, plug a
different lamp into the same outlet. If
the second lamp works, you've just
cleared the outlet, the wiring, and the
power to the house in one move, and the
problem lives in the first lamp. If it
doesn't work, the first lamp is cleared
instead. Engineers call this the
half-split method, and the US Air Force
was already studying it as a way to
train technicians in the 1950s.
Each good test cuts the problem in half.
10 good tests can narrow a thousand
possibilities down to one. Principle
three, start with something you're
allowed to break. Find an old remote, a
dead toaster, a cheap gadget nobody will
miss. Keep it unplugged, and leave
microwaves and old televisions to
professionals because they can hold a
dangerous charge even when they're
switched off. Open it, photograph every
step, try to name every part and say
what it does. You don't even have to fix
it. The goal is to put one more working
model in your head and to feel in your
body that opening something is
survivable. Every fixer you've ever
admired started with a pile of things
they were allowed to ruin. When this
kind of mind matures, it stops being
only about machines. The seasoned fixer
starts to see how everything holds
together. Why a morning routine falls
apart at the same step every day. Why a
team keeps missing the same deadline.
The skill grows from taking things apart
into understanding how things stay
together. And the wisest fixers learn
the hardest lesson of all. When to put
the screwdriver down. Before they start,
they ask, "Do you want help fixing this
or do you just want me here?" That one
question turns a mind built for repair
into a person people feel safe around.
Someone who can fix almost anything and
who knows that some things only need to
be held. In 1974, Robert Pirsig
published a book about a long motorcycle
trip and the quiet discipline of keeping
a machine running. Tucked inside it is a
line fixers understand before anyone
else. The real cycle you're working on
is a cycle called yourself. Kenneth
Craik believed the mind carries a small
working model of the world. Maybe the
most important model any of us ever
builds is the one of ourselves. And
maybe the same rules apply. Run it
